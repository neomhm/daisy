Orchid

Orchid 1.1 · trained

Finds the facts in your documents

Hand Orchid your documents, such as invoices, brochures, price lists, web pages or registration papers. She reads them one chunk at a time and marks the facts: your business's name, address, phone, e-mail, website, opening hours, founding date and more, 25 kinds in all. She also says what kind of document it is and what language it's in. Orchid never writes a word: she only marks what is already there, so every value she returns is a slice of your own document. Code then checks each value and keeps its source.

Final results. Trained further on real web pages of 196 businesses in the US and France, then tested on real pages she never saw.

Tulip 1.1 · trained

Spreadsheet importer

Hand Tulip a spreadsheet and she reads its preview, then writes a short TulipScript program that imports every row into the right one of your business tables, or refuses the sheet. That program never runs as raw code: a locked-down interpreter checks every instruction. If a sheet doesn't fit, Tulip refuses cleanly. No guessing, no silent damage.

Jasmine

Jasmine 1c · being retrained

Dresses the site to fit the business

Jasmine never sees your pictures. Code reads your documents and photos and turns them into a few plain signals: what you sell (pizza, vegan, sushi, bakery), the mood of your words (fresh, rustic, elegant), and how your photos feel (bright or dark, warm or cool, calm or vivid). From these, Jasmine picks the look, the font pair, the background, the main and accent colours, and their strength. A pizzeria gets warm reds and terracotta, a vegan kitchen fresh greens, and a sushi bar calm tones on a neutral background. Your logo, when you have one, stays the main colour. Code turns her choices into exact colours and checks every contrast pair, so she cannot make an unreadable site.

Daisy

Daisy 1.4 · trained

Answers questions about your own data

Ask in plain English, the way you would type it: “how many croissants have we got left?”, “who hasn't been in since June?”, “what's our busiest day?”. Daisy turns the question into a precise query spec in about 0.3 seconds on one CPU core. Code checks the spec, compiles it to SQL for SQLite, PostgreSQL or MySQL, and runs it read-only. You then see the exact rows and the SQL behind the answer. No GPU is needed. When she doesn't know, she says so. When she isn't sure, she asks you back with a few choices.

Two blind tests of questions she never saw: 118 owners' questions (93 to answer, 25 to refuse), written by an assistant, and a fresh test of 113 other questions (74 to answer, 39 to refuse) for six other businesses, written by a different model. Nothing in her training was shaped by them.

Magnolia

Magnolia · in preparation

Reads a database she has never seen, and tells Daisy what everything in it means

Every business keeps its data its own way. One calls its customers “clients”, another “Kunden”, another CUSTNO; a price may sit in a column called montant_ttc, prix or fld_07. Magnolia reads a database once, when it is connected: the names of its tables and columns, their types and a few sample values. For each table she says which kind of business record it holds (products, bookings, invoices, customers, opening hours and so on), and for each column what it contains, such as “the price, tax included” or “the date the booking was made”, each with her confidence. Daisy then answers the owner's questions using that reading.

  • Once per database, not per question, so Daisy stays under one second an answer.
  • The owner sees her reading when the database is connected, and can correct it once for good.
  • When she is not sure, she leaves a column unread rather than guess: a wrong reading would repeat on every question.
  • She works for the whole team: Bouquet and Tulip use the same reading.

Not trained yet. Her design was approved on 30 September 2026, and her training follows Daisy 2.0's pretraining, which she is built on. The bars below are plain code with no model: the gap she is trained to close.

Why she is needed: today Daisy reads PLAN's own standard tables only. On a fresh test of 10 businesses whose databases use other names (French, German, Italian, Spanish and legacy codes), Daisy 1.4 could not answer any question: she stopped at the unknown table names before reading a single one. Magnolia removes that wall.

What she will learn from: the schemas of Spider and BIRD, two open research collections (CC BY-SA 4.0) whose columns come with plain-English names and descriptions, credited in her model file; and our own generated business databases (bakeries, restaurants, salons, trades and shops) with every table and column renamed at random: abbreviations (cust_nm), other languages (Kunde, importe, fatturato), capitals and separators (CUSTOMER_NAME) and legacy codes (ZZ_CUSTNO), so she reads a name and its values instead of memorising names. A column called fld_07 holding 2024-03-01 is clearly a date. Never used: an owner's own data, or anything from the frozen tests that judge her and Daisy; a leak check refuses them automatically.

In-house, no outside model weights: about 1 to 3 million parameters as extra layers on Daisy 2.0's own backbone. One pretraining and one vocabulary serve both, within Daisy's size limit of about 55M parameters. Code checks types and value shapes around her, and a reading below her confidence threshold is not used.

Iris

Iris · in preparation

Watches every site PLAN puts online, tests what matters most, and gets any problem fixed

When a site goes live, Iris goes over all of it, the way a careful customer would, and again every day after that:

  • Every page opens, and every link, picture and file answers.
  • Every price and every fact shown is exactly the one the owner approved.
  • The legal pages are there, and the site is secure (HTTPS).
  • The owner's login works, and a wrong password is refused.
  • An order goes through from start to finish, for sites that take orders: Iris places a test order, then cancels it.
  • The contact details, opening hours and allergens are shown.
  • Backups exist, and one really restores.
  • Anyone can use the site: readable contrast, keyboard use and speed.

Not trained or live yet. Named and approved on 30 September 2026: her first hand-written checks and her training preparation start now.

When she finds a problem, she reports it to Bouquet with the evidence, and Bouquet calls the right teammate: Orchid for a fact read wrong, Tulip for a table imported wrong, Jasmine for a design that fails its checks, Daisy or Magnolia for a wrong answer or a wrong reading of a database. Or Bouquet simply has the site rebuilt. Every fix is checked again by Iris before it goes live. If something critical breaks on the live site (orders not working, a wrong price, the site down), the last good version comes back at once, then the repair begins. The owner decides anything about their own data, prices or legal texts: Iris never changes those on her own. After a few failed repairs, the owner is told plainly what is wrong, and always sees a simple health line: all green, or what was fixed and when.

What she will learn from: PLAN's own generated sites, rendered as phones and computers see them, with faults planted on purpose: garbled text, a wrong language, text cut off or overlapping, empty sections, missing pictures, prices written for the wrong country. Faults and businesses she never saw in training are held out for her test, frozen before training starts. Never used: owners' real sites, or other companies' websites.

Her targets, before she is used: the hand-written checks catch every planted fault of the kinds they cover, with no false alarm on a correct site; the model finds at least 90% of the held-out faults, with at most 2% false alarms on correct pages, on one ordinary computer core. Next: the repair loop through Bouquet, and the test order once PLAN's back end (orders, bookings, the owner's login) is built.

Mostly checks written by hand, one list per feature of the site, each tested like the site's own components. Independent of whatever built the site: she compares the live site with what the owner approved, never with the builder's own output, so a mistake cannot hide by agreeing with itself. A small in-house model only for what code cannot judge: whether a page reads right (garbled characters, the wrong language, placeholder text left behind, text cut off or overlapping, a section drawn empty). She never writes code: her repairs are re-running a step, rebuilding, publishing again, or putting the last good version back.
